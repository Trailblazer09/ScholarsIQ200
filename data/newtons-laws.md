# Newton's Laws of Motion

## Introduction
Sir Isaac Newton's three laws of motion, published in 1687, form the foundation of classical mechanics. They describe the relationship between the motion of an object and the forces acting on it. These laws accurately describe the motion of everyday objects at speeds far below the speed of light.

## First Law: The Law of Inertia
Newton's first law states that an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted upon by an unbalanced external force. This property of objects to resist changes in their state of motion is called inertia. A book on a table remains still until pushed; a hockey puck slides until friction stops it. Inertia depends on mass: the greater the mass, the greater the inertia.

## Second Law: Force, Mass and Acceleration
Newton's second law states that the acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass. It is expressed by the equation F = m × a, where F is force in newtons, m is mass in kilograms, and a is acceleration in metres per second squared. This means a larger force produces a larger acceleration, while a more massive object accelerates less for the same force.

## Third Law: Action and Reaction
Newton's third law states that for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. When one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object exerts a force of equal magnitude but opposite direction on the first. Examples include a rocket expelling gas downward to push itself upward, and a swimmer pushing water backward to move forward. The two forces act on different objects and therefore do not cancel out.

## Applications
Newton's laws explain everyday phenomena and are used to design vehicles, structures, and spacecraft. Seatbelts work because of inertia (first law); engineers use F = ma to calculate forces (second law); and rockets and jets rely on action-reaction (third law).
