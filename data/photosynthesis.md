# Photosynthesis

## Overview
Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. It is the foundation of most food chains on Earth and is responsible for producing the oxygen in our atmosphere. The overall reaction can be summarised as: six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six molecules of water, in the presence of light energy and chlorophyll, produce one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen.

## The Light-Dependent Reactions
The light-dependent reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast. Chlorophyll and other pigments absorb light energy, which excites electrons to a higher energy level. This energy is used to split water molecules (photolysis), releasing oxygen as a by-product. The energy is captured in two energy-carrying molecules: ATP and NADPH. These reactions require light to proceed and cannot occur in darkness.

## The Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent Reactions)
The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma of the chloroplast and does not directly require light. It uses the ATP and NADPH produced in the light-dependent reactions to fix carbon dioxide into glucose. The enzyme RuBisCO catalyses the first major step, attaching CO2 to a five-carbon sugar. Through a series of reactions, the cycle regenerates its starting molecule and produces glucose that the plant uses for energy and growth.

## Factors Affecting the Rate
Three main factors affect the rate of photosynthesis: light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, and temperature. As light intensity increases, the rate increases until it plateaus when another factor becomes limiting. Higher CO2 concentrations generally increase the rate up to a point. Temperature affects the enzymes involved; too low and reactions slow, too high and enzymes denature. These are known as limiting factors.

## Why It Matters
Photosynthesis underpins nearly all life on Earth. It removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and releases oxygen, playing a central role in the carbon cycle and climate regulation. Understanding photosynthesis is essential in biology, agriculture, and environmental science.
